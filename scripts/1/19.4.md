Over 84 percent of the taxes the US federal government collects come from paychecks like yours. So if AI wipes out most of those paychecks before 2036, who's going to pay off $40.5 trillion in national debt?

 

On October 29, 2026, Elon Musk posted on X that once super-intelligent machines create abundance, saving money for the distant future is pointless.  You can try forwarding that to your landlord on the first of the month and see how it goes.

 

The problem with it is that your mortgage still assumes you get paid on Friday. The government bonds your bank is holding assume the same thing, because Washington pays those back out of taxes on people's paychecks. Right now American households owe $18.8 trillion, most of it in mortgages with fixed monthly payments, and those payments don't care what the robots are doing.

 

Back in July 2026, Musk told The Economist that money stops mattering by 2036. That's the same year the Congressional Budget Office projects interest on America's debt will pass $2.1 trillion a year. So right when money is supposed to stop mattering, Washington owes more than two trillion dollars a year in interest, and the people holding those bonds still expect to get paid in dollars.

You've probably got an answer for this one already: tax the robots and mail everyone a check. I don't think that survives the Treasury's own numbers (I'll show you why in a minute), and it's only one of the fixes people are talking about.

 

People have floated three escape hatches, and each one breaks somewhere different. One, the government mails everyone a check, but one governor with a veto pen can cut that check in half. Two, you get paid in AI computing power instead of dollars, but that gets cheaper every year, so it can lose its value before rent is due. Three, the government just prints the money. You can print dollars, but you can't print power plants, and every new dollar ends up bidding for the same electricity as the data centers.

 

So when the Friday paycheck stops, what happens to your money, and who gets to decide what shows up in your bank account every month?

 

I'll start with the best argument Silicon Valley has, which is that AI makes almost everything cheaper, because on prices I actually think the engineers are right.

 

Take a $120 pair of running shoes, or a routine dental X-ray. Most of what you're paying for is human time: somebody stitched the shoe and somebody drove the truck, and somebody else pulled the box off a warehouse shelf and ran the payroll.

 

Now swap out eighty percent of those hours for robots and software that cost roughly what the electricity costs. Making things gets dramatically cheaper and competition drags prices down right after it. On a whiteboard in Palo Alto, that's abundance.

 

But the thing is, that whiteboard hasn't met a thirty-year loan yet.

Let's Imagine it's 2025 and we are in austin, taxas. we have claire here who is a software developer and she just locked in a mortgage at $3,400 per month. Five years later, coding agents have cut grocery and electronics prices in half. That's great, right! But they've also cut her freelance income by seventy percent, and it doesn't matter how cheap things get if she just doesn't have the thirty-four hundred dollars.

 

But the bank doesn’t care. They want $3,400 every single month until 2055, or the sheriff will show up to change the locks. And no, she can’t just wait for the Fed to cut rates. Banks lend money based on income and hers just dropped 70%, while her house is actively losing value. It’s the exact same trap that swallowed millions of homeowners back in 2009.

 

Hang on to Claire, by the way, because when we get to 2036 we're going to run her house through all three escape hatches.

 

Irving Fisher watched this wreck families in the 1930s and called it debt deflation. It's when wages and prices fall together but the old debts don't move at all, so every dollar you owe gets heavier right as the money coming in dries up.

 

And you can already see the start of it in the government's labor data. Since 2017 the amount workers produce per hour has gone up twenty percent while their pay hasn't kept up, which has pushed the share of the economy going to workers to its lowest point since the government started tracking it in 1947, back when Harry Truman was president.

 

So where's the money going instead? Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft are putting over $740 billion a year into AI infrastructure, past $820 billion if you add Oracle. They're spending so fast that Alphabet just posted its first quarters of negative free Cash flow, meaning it spent more cash than the business brought in, and Amazon's has been negative for the past twelve months.

 

I know what you're thinking: these companies are pulling in trillions, so why not just tax the server farms?

 

Pulling up Table 9 of the Monthly Treasury Statement shows you exactly where Washington gets its money. Back in 2024, income and payroll taxes from regular people made up 84.1 percent of all federal tax revenue, and the 2026 numbers push that past 86. Corporations only chipped in 10.8 percent. And the CBO expects that corporate share to drop to around 7, mostly because a 2025 tax law lets companies write off their servers and chips the same year they buy them. Every dollar they spend on hardware comes straight off the profit they owe taxes on.

 

That's seven percent from companies against more than 84 from people's paychecks.

 

If AI replaces trillions in wages, that money doesn't just disappear. It turns into corporate profit. The problem is that wages and profits don't get taxed the same way. When you earn a paycheck, the government takes its cut off the top through withholding and payroll tax before the money ever hits your bank account. Corporations only get taxed on whatever's left after deductions, and they can shift those profits to low-tax countries like Ireland. Meanwhile, the people who own the company can just borrow against their stock instead of selling it, which means they don't trigger a tax bill at all. So basically, every dollar that moves from your paycheck to a company's bottom line moves from the heaviest tax bracket in America to one of the lightest.

 

Then add the falling prices we just talked about. Companies sell things for fewer dollars, so their profits shrink in dollar terms too, but the $40.5 trillion doesn't shrink, because we borrowed it at the old prices.

 

Go a few rows down the same table and you'll see interest on the national debt has already passed the entire defense budget, at over $1 trillion a year. So when AI cuts into wages, it's cutting into the one tax base big enough to keep paying that debt, and to me that's the whole problem sitting in one table.

 

Which brings us to escape hatch number one, the government check.

 

The idea's simple enough. You tax the hardware, borrow the difference and mail everyone a monthly dividend. Musk calls it universal high income, and Sam Altman's version is an American Equity Fund that would tax big companies on their stock and land and pay every adult a yearly check.

 

The plumbing's already there, too. In 2020, stimulus payments landed in tens of millions of bank accounts within weeks, so getting the money to you isn't the hard part. The hard part is what happens when the government runs short.

 

Think about how working people won the forty-hour week and steady raises in the last century. When dockworkers in Rotterdam or autoworkers in Detroit walked off the job, ships sat at the pier and assembly lines went quiet, and by Friday the companies were bleeding cash. Owners shared the gains because they had to, since the machines stopped the minute the workers did.

 

Altight, now picture the same strike in 2034. The ports are automated and the trucks drive themselves, so a million people can march outside the capitol demanding a bigger check while the cranes keep running and the servers keep going at ninety-nine percent uptime.

 

Nothing stops.

 

You'd still have your vote, so the political pressure doesn't go away. What goes away is your bargaining power at work. Once the economy can run without your labor, your income isn't something you negotiate anymore, it's whatever amount politicians leave in the budget when money gets tight.

 

Remember that veto pen? Alaska found out what one can do in June 2016.

 

Since 1982, every Alaska resident, newborns included, has gotten a yearly check from the state's oil wealth. It's called the Permanent Fund Dividend, and for more than thirty years politicians treated it like it was untouchable.

 

Then oil revenue dropped and the state found a $4 billion hole in its budget. Under the formula on the books, each resident was owed $2,052, which is $8,208 for a family of four heading into winter. Governor Bill Walker took out his veto pen and cut every check in half, down to $1,022.

 

With one signature, a family lost over four thousand dollars, right in October when people are trying to buy heating fuel for the winter.

Some lawmakers actually sued the governor over it. The case was *Wielechowski v. State*, and the Alaska Supreme Court said, no, the governor's allowed to do that, because the dividend isn't in the state constitution. It's just regular budget money that gets voted on every year, so whenever bondholders and state agencies need to get paid first, politicians can cut the check.

Well, I know the obvious pushback here is that Alaska only ran out of money because oil crashed, and an AI boom isn't a crash.

Except look at what happens to Washington's money in an AI boom. Even if the economy's growing, if people's paychecks shrink, the main thing Washington taxes shrinks with them, right when the CBO says interest on the debt is heading toward $2 trillion a year. And cutting interest rates doesn't get them out of it either, because sure, lower rates make the interest bill smaller, but falling prices also mean the government collects fewer tax dollars. So compared to what Washington's actually bringing in, that debt keeps getting heavier. And just like Alaska, Washington has to pay its bondholders first, because if it doesn't, it defaults. So the check going to you is what gets cut.

By 2025, Alaska's formula said every resident was supposed to get about $3,900 but They got $1,000.

 

So I think this is what living on a government dividend looks like: your rent is always one budget fight away from getting cut in half.

 

Some people in Silicon Valley looked at all this and decided politicians make bad paymasters, which gets us to escape hatch number two. Skip the government entirely and pay people in AI computing power, meaning the processing time that AI models run on.

 

In May 2024, on the All-In podcast, Sam Altman floated an idea he called Universal Basic Compute. Instead of waiting on Congress to send dollars, every adult would get a monthly slice of GPT-7's computing power that they could use, rent out or sell for cash on an open exchange.

 

And when you look at what companies are spending on AI right now, you can see the appeal.

 

In early 2026, Forbes reported that Uber burned through its whole annual AI budget four months into a twelve-month year. Five thousand engineers had started using coding agents, and the company's CTO ended up capping token spending at $1,500 per engineer per month. (Tokens are basically how AI companies bill you, chunk of text by chunk of text.) Over at Nvidia, Jensen Huang said engineers' token budgets should be worth about half their base salary. With demand like that, a monthly slice of compute sounds a lot like owning an oil well.

 

So why does it break?

 

look at why Uber's bill got so high. Since 2023, faster chips have actually pushed the price of a single token down by about ninety percent. Companies are spending more in total only because their engineers are running millions more tasks than they used to, while the tokens themselves keep getting cheaper. And If you're the one getting paid in it, your paycheck is losing value the whole time.

 

Nvidia ships a new generation of AI chips about every year, so today's compute keeps losing value, and compute you don't use is gone the second it passes, kind of like an empty seat on a flight. You can't save Tuesday's idle processing in a jar and spend it on December's heating bill.

 

Now picture the first of the month in 2033. Tens of millions of renters need dollars by 9 AM for rent and property tax, so they all dump their compute on the exchange in the same minute, and the price crashes right when the bills are due.

 

To keep that crash from happening, the platforms have every reason to hold your credits inside their own walls. Instead of letting you cash out into dollars, they'd let you spend your balance on their partner housing and rides and deliveries.

If you're wondering how that's any different from regular money, think about how airline miles work.

Imagine a thousand dollars in  your checking account on the one hand and a thousand dollars in airline miles in the other. The first pays for anything you need; groceries, a mortgage, a startup or a simple transfer. And the second is Airline miles which only work in an airport. While a thousand dollars might buy you airline miles, the thousand dollars in airline miles wil definitely not cover for anything else.  

Coal miners in West Virginia got paid exactly like that a century ago.

 

Companies like "Island Creek Coal" paid workers in brass tokens and paper scrip that only worked at the company store and for company rent, and they wouldn't buy it back for cash. If a miner needed real dollars, say for a train ticket out of the valley, outside traders would buy his scrip off him, usually at a discount of a quarter or more.

 

It was a wage built to keep you from leaving. After armed strikes broke out along Paint Creek in 1912, states started tightening their wage laws, and in 1938 Congress used the Fair Labor Standards Act to ban, nationwide, company scrip that couldn't be traded for cash.

 

Now put that system on a phone. In late 2020, city governments in Shenzhen and Suzhou tested digital yuan vouchers that expired after six days. They only worked at roughly three thousand approved stores, and if you didn't spend them in time, they just vanished.

Now let a private tech company run that kind of wallet. You pay zero fees as long as you live in their partner apartments and order from their warehouses, but the moment you move money to an outside bank to pay a regular mortgage, there's a twenty percent conversion fee.

 

That's a digital company town, and I don't think anyone would vote for one on purpose.

 

Which leaves escape hatch number three: what if the Fed and the Treasury just print the money?

 

Say taxes fall short and private scrip gets banned. Congress can tell the central bank to create digital dollars and drop them into every checking account each month, and making those dollars costs nothing, since it's just a number on a government server. What nobody can print is copper wire, transformers or power plants.

 

Software copies itself for pennies, so it's easy to forget how much physical energy sits behind the outlet. A phone charger draws about ten watts. A microwave on full blast pulls a thousand, which is one kilowatt, and if you ran a million microwaves nonstop, all day and all night, you'd be at one gigawatt, roughly the steady power draw of every home in a major city. NextEra Energy says single AI campuses are now asking for up to five gigawatts. That's five major cities' worth of homes, for one campus.

 

In 2024, data centers worldwide used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity, and the International Energy Agency projects 945 by 2030, which is more than all of Japan uses in a year.

 

Ireland's data centers already use over twenty-one percent of the country's metered power, more than every urban home in Ireland combined. In West London, new housing projects along the M4 corridor got frozen because nearby data centers had already reserved the local substation's power into the 2030s.

 

So how does any of that break a printed check? Picture an auction for electricity.

 

PJM runs the grid for thirteen states, from Illinois to New Jersey, and it holds an auction where power plants get paid to promise electricity for the years ahead. In its 2025/2026 auction the price jumped from $28.92 to $269.92 per megawatt-day. That's up 833 percent, and the cost lands on household power bills. Around the same time, Microsoft signed a twenty-year deal to buy the entire output of the restarted Three Mile Island reactor, which means one customer gets a whole nuclear reactor.

 

Even if Washington prints trillions of dollars and drops a check in your account every month, that fixed check is going up against trillion-dollar companies that are already outbidding everyone for megawatts and transformers, and locking up the land to put them on.

 

When there's plenty of cash but the physical stuff hits a limit, money stops doing its job, because having it doesn't mean you can buy anything with it.

 

We saw how this works in London on December first, 1941. German submarines were sinking merchant ships across the Atlantic, and at the same time, wartime overtime had put extra cash in British workers’ pockets. If shoppers could bid with pounds alone, the richest households would have emptied the shops by noon, so the Ministry of Food gave every person the same sixteen points every four weeks.

 

Even when families still have cash left over, once their sixteen points run out at the counter, those pounds can’t buy a single tin of food, because the government’s point system now controls what their money is allowed to buy.

 

Okay, remember Claire? Jump ahead with me to October 2036.

 

None of this happens overnight. It's a ten-year squeeze, and by October 2036 Claire still has nineteen years of $3,400 payments left on her house. It's the year Musk says money stops mattering and the year CBO expects interest on the debt to pass $2.1 trillion, Obviously I don't know exactly what 2036 is going to look like, but we look at the risk she runs under each one. so let's run her through the hatches.

 

Say she's counting on the government dividend. With Washington sending over two trillion dollars a year to bondholders while paycheck taxes shrink, all it takes is one budget standoff to cut her check in half, the way Alaska did in 2016.

Or say she's paid in compute credits. If tokens drop ninety percent and the platform charges a twenty percent fee to cash out of its wallet, there's a real chance she comes up short of the dollars her bank wants on the first of the month.

Printing the cash is the one option that actually helps with the mortgage, because her $3,400 payment is fixed, so cheaper dollars make it easier to cover and the bank leaves her alone. Even then, with all those new dollars bidding for the same limited power as trillion-dollar server farms, she could end up keeping the house and not being able to afford the air conditioning inside it.

 

For about two hundred years, the paycheck was an unwritten agreement between regular people and the people running things. Factories and finance ministries couldn't run a single day without workers showing up, so they had to pay in cash you could save or invest, or use to walk away from a bad deal.

 

By 2036 they won't need you to show up, and putting a big number on your phone screen won't cost them anything.

 

I think that's the part people are missing. If you can't negotiate a check, you can't call it income.

 

That is an allowance.
